Denoising Methods

Original

Gaussian Blur

One-Step Denoise

Euler Denoise


One-step denoising is not enough because from immense noise, the predicted velocity is the average of all possibilities. Going incrementally allows the denosing step to move towards more specific predictions. The predicted velocity can change along the path. To walk through the denoising incrementally, Euler integration is used to sample. When comparing the denoising methods, we could see the Gaussian blur be very less detailed the rest with grainy noise still visible. We could see the Euler denoising be more detailed than the One-step denoising.

Visual Anagrams


a small island with a lighthouse in an ocean

a hot air balloon flying in the cloudy sky



a painting of a sunny tropical beach

a painting of a campfire



To create a visual anagram, instead of simply using the classifer-free guidance velocity by itself, we take the average of two CFG velocities. One is from the upright version of the image, and the other is the upside-down version of the image. Each are conditioned on separate prompts.

Hybrid Images


a photo of flowers

a photo of a peacock



a watercolor of flowers

a watercolor of a duck



To create a hybrid image, the predicted velocity used to steer the direction of denoising is the sum of the low pass frequencies of the CFG velocity of one prompt and the high pass frequencies of the CFG velocity of another prompt.

For both epoch observations, we can observe that the image we get after the one-step denoise attempt looks fairly the same regardless of the noise input. This is because when using a model that is trained to minimize the sum squared distance to all training examples, the model determines that the most optimal denosing step is one that reduces the distance equally to all, essentially making the denoising attempt reach the average of all the digits it trains on. This is clear to see because the result we see for all the denoise attempts is the same amalgamation that looks like the average of all digits.
